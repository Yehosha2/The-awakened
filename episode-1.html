Hebrew.

This is not simply the story of a book.

CHAPTER ONE

THE MOTHER OF LIFE

VISUAL — ANCIENT MESOPOTAMIA. RIVERS. CLAY TABLETS.
NARRATOR Long before the Hebrew Bible was written in the form we know today, civilizations of Mesopotamia were already recording stories about creation.

Among the figures we encounter is Nintur, also associated in different traditions with names such as Ninmah and Aruru.

She appears within Mesopotamian traditions as a powerful mother or creator figure.

But we have to be careful.

The ancient texts do not give us one single universal Mesopotamian creation story.

Different cities preserved different traditions. Different gods appear in different texts. Different stories were written at different times.

The question is not simply which story came first, but how these traditions developed and interacted.
CHAPTER TWO

ZIUSUDRA, ATRAHASIS, AND NOAH

VISUAL — A CLAY TABLET. RAIN BEGINS FALLING.
NARRATOR Then comes one of humanity's most enduring stories:

The Flood.

In the Sumerian tradition, we encounter Ziusudra.

In Akkadian tradition, Atrahasis.

In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Utnapishtim.

And in Genesis: Noah.

These stories contain striking similarities: a divinely sent flood, a chosen survivor, a vessel, preservation of life, and the survival of humanity.

But they are not identical stories.

The differences matter.

CHAPTER THREE

GENESIS

VISUAL — HEBREW LETTERS FORM THE OPENING OF GENESIS.
NARRATOR Then we arrive at Genesis.

The beginning.

Creation.

Adam.

Eve.

Abraham.

Jacob.

But Genesis did not survive in only one textual tradition.

The Hebrew Bible was transmitted through multiple textual traditions, including the tradition behind the later Masoretic Text, the Greek Septuagint, and the Samaritan Pentateuch.

CHAPTER FOUR

THE SAMARITANS

VISUAL — MOUNT GERIZIM. ANCIENT SAMARITAN SCRIPT.
NARRATOR The Samaritans preserve a distinct ancient Israelite religious tradition.

Their sacred scripture is the Pentateuch: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy.

Their ancient script developed from the Paleo-Hebrew tradition.

Their theology places special importance on Mount Gerizim.

The Dead Sea Scrolls later revealed that some Pentateuchal manuscripts contain textual features resembling the Samaritan Pentateuch.

The Samaritan textual tradition reaches much deeper into antiquity than its surviving medieval manuscripts.
CHAPTER FIVE

THE SCROLLS

VISUAL — QUMRAN CAVES. SCROLL FRAGMENTS.
NARRATOR Then came one of the greatest archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century.

The Dead Sea Scrolls.

Discovered beginning in 1947, they contained thousands of fragments representing hundreds of manuscripts.

Some date from the third century BCE. Others reach into the first century CE.

They preserve biblical texts, religious writings, legal traditions, prayers, commentary, and other literature from the world surrounding the time of Yehoshua.

The ancient Bible was no longer only something we read. We could touch pieces of the world that produced it.
CHAPTER SIX

YEHOSHUA

VISUAL — ANCIENT JUDEA. JERUSALEM.
NARRATOR Then we arrive at the first century.

A Jewish teacher appears in Galilee.

His name is represented in ancient languages in forms related to Yeshua.

In Greek: Iēsous.

In English: Jesus.

In the form we have been using throughout our study: Yehoshua.

The New Testament presents him within the world of Second Temple Judaism.

He speaks about Torah. The Kingdom of God. Repentance. Mercy. Prayer. Love of God. Love of neighbor. And resurrection.

CHAPTER SEVEN

THE EARLIEST CHRISTIAN VOICES

VISUAL — GREEK MANUSCRIPT.
NARRATOR Before the great medieval cathedrals... before European paintings of Christ... there were small communities meeting in homes.

Letters circulated.

Stories were copied.

Teachings were remembered.

Paul's letters are among the earliest surviving Christian writings.

Then came the Gospels.

Later came writings such as the Epistle of Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas.

Their visions, teachings, and parables give us a glimpse into the diversity of early Christian thought.

CHAPTER EIGHT

THE IMAGE OF YEHOSHUA

VISUAL — EARLY CHRISTIAN ART.
NARRATOR For centuries after Yehoshua lived, Christians did not possess a photograph of him.

There is no surviving photograph.

There is no authenticated contemporary portrait that can be proven to be his actual face.

The earliest surviving Christian images are artistic and symbolic.

One important early Christian site is Dura-Europos, where paintings from around the third century CE include scenes associated with Christ.

Centuries later came the famous Christ Pantocrator icon from Sinai, dating to approximately the sixth century.

These are windows into how different Christian communities imagined Yehoshua—not photographs recovered from antiquity.
CHAPTER NINE

THE TWELVE

VISUAL — TWELVE POINTS OF LIGHT.
NARRATOR Before Yehoshua, Israel's identity had already been organized around the descendants of Jacob.

Jacob is given the name: Israel.

His sons become associated with the tribes of Israel.

Reuben. Simeon. Levi. Judah. Dan. Naphtali. Gad. Asher. Issachar. Zebulun. Joseph. Benjamin.

The biblical tradition later speaks of the tribes in different configurations.

But the number twelve becomes one of the great symbolic structures of Israelite identity.

And Yehoshua's choosing of twelve apostles echoes that symbolism.

CHAPTER TEN

WHAT WAS REALLY WRITTEN?

VISUAL — MANUSCRIPT FRAGMENTS SCATTERED ACROSS A TABLE.
NARRATOR This is where our journey becomes difficult.

We do not possess the original manuscripts of the books of the Bible.

We possess copies.

Fragments.

Quotations.

Translations.

Ancient versions.

And manuscripts separated from their authors by centuries.

The manuscript tradition is not a straight line. It is more like a river.

Branches separate. Some disappear. Others survive.

Eventually those surviving branches become the texts we know.

CHAPTER ELEVEN

WHAT WE KNOW

VISUAL — THREE WORDS APPEAR: TRADITION. EVIDENCE. INTERPRETATION.
NARRATOR There are three things we must never confuse.
What the ancient text says.
What later tradition says about the text.
What archaeology can actually demonstrate.

If an ancient manuscript says something, we can report it.

If a religious tradition interprets that passage, we can explain the interpretation.

But if archaeology has not demonstrated something, we should not pretend that it has.

The search for truth requires both faith and honesty.
